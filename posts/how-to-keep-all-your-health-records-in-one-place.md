Most people don't realize how scattered their medical history is until they need it. You're filling out a new patient form, or trying to remember which year you had that procedure, or trying to send a specialist your latest labs — and suddenly you're combing through three patient portals, an old email thread, a folder of PDFs, and the back of a bathroom drawer.

Health records weren't designed to be portable. For most of modern medicine, they belonged to whoever ran the office or hospital where you were seen. That's been changing quickly, and 2026 is one of the better years on record to actually pull your health history together in one place. The federal patient access rules are mature, more hospitals expose your records through standard APIs, and Apple, OneRecord, Sync.MD, and AI health companions like heymedy have all built around the same idea: your records should follow you.

But "in one place" can mean a few different things, and the right setup depends on what you're trying to do. Here's a practical look.

## First, Decide What You Actually Need

Before downloading another app, it helps to be honest about why you want this in the first place. People usually want one of three things, and they pull toward different tools.

You might want a clean clinical archive — labs, imaging reports, visit summaries, vaccinations, all stored and searchable. You might want a living, ongoing log — what you took today, what symptoms you noticed this week, how you're trending month over month. Or you might want help making sense of it all — a friendly second pair of eyes that can read a confusing report and explain what it means in plain English.

Most people want all three. Few apps do all three well. The smart move is to pair tools rather than expect one to do everything.

## Apple Health Records: The Free Default

If you have an iPhone, Apple Health is the obvious starting point — and it's better than people give it credit for. Apple Health Records pulls clinical data directly from more than 800 institutions across the U.S., Canada, and the U.K. using FHIR, the federal health data standard. When it works, you log in once with your patient portal credentials and your conditions, medications, labs, immunizations, procedures, and vitals start flowing in.

Where it falls short: Apple Health stores the data, but it doesn't really interpret it. You'll see your lab values, but not what they mean for you. You also can't bring in records from outside the network — if your doctor is at a small clinic that isn't connected to FHIR yet, you're back to PDFs and faxes.

Free, private, and a strong foundation. Pair it with something that can actually do something with what's there.

## OneRecord: Strongest Pure Aggregator

OneRecord is built around one job: pulling your records out of every connected hospital, clinic, and health plan in the U.S. and putting them in a single searchable vault. It uses FHIR APIs and connects to the country's largest health information networks (CareQuality and CommonWell), which means it can often find records from places Apple Health can't.

The patient app is free. You can request, store, and share records with doctors or family, and view a consolidated timeline of your blood pressure, labs, medications, and procedures over time.

OneRecord is what you want if your priority is getting everything into one secure archive. It's lighter on the day-to-day tracking and AI explanation side, but its aggregation is hard to beat.

## Sync.MD: Records Plus an AI Assistant

Sync.MD is a free personal health record app that pairs record consolidation with an AI assistant called Sync.Sense. You can pull records in from connected portals like MyChart, upload PDFs, and then ask the AI to translate medical jargon, generate visit summaries, or help you prep for an appointment. It even has a Guest Mode so you can try the assistant before committing to an account.

It's genuinely useful for understanding what's in your file. The trade-off is that Sync.MD is more of a records-and-prep tool than a daily health tracker. It shines if you have a lot of paperwork to wrangle and want help reading it.

## heymedy: A Living Health Companion

heymedy is built for the part of your health that lives between appointments. You can bring in your records — lab PDFs, doctor's notes, your medication list — and heymedy keeps them organized alongside the things you log day to day: symptoms, side effects, how you're sleeping, when you took what.

Where heymedy is different is in how it uses that combined picture. Because it sees both your clinical history and your daily reality, it can answer questions in context — like helping you understand a result before your next appointment, or surfacing a pattern worth flagging with your doctor — instead of guessing the way a generic chatbot would.

heymedy is best when you want a single place that holds your records and helps you understand and act on them over time. If you only want a static archive, OneRecord may be lighter. If you want ongoing company through the day-to-day, heymedy fits.

## How to Actually Set This Up

A workflow that tends to hold up:

Start with Apple Health Records. Connect every hospital and clinic you've been to in the last few years that's listed in the directory. This is your baseline.

Add an aggregator if you have records outside the Apple network. OneRecord is the most thorough; Sync.MD is a strong alternative if you also want AI help reading your file.

Layer on a daily companion if your health is something you're actively managing. heymedy is built for this — pulling in your records and then walking with you through the day-to-day questions that come up. Some people just keep a notes file. Either way, the goal is to capture what's happening in real time, because the gaps between visits are usually where context gets lost.

Once a year, do a sweep. Make sure new providers are connected, your medication list reflects reality, and old records you no longer need are archived.

## The Real Win

Keeping your records in one place isn't really about the records. It's about walking into appointments knowing what you know, and walking out with answers that actually go somewhere instead of getting lost in the next pile of paperwork.

The tools to do this are finally good. The question is which combination fits your life.

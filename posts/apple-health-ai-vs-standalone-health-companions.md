Apple is about to change the game for health tracking on your iPhone. With iOS 26.4 expected this spring, the Health app is getting its biggest overhaul in years — an AI health coach, built-in nutrition tracking, expert health videos, and a redesigned interface. If you already carry an iPhone, you're about to get a lot more health insight without installing anything new.

So does that mean standalone health apps are finished? Not quite. Here's what's actually coming, what it's missing, and how to think about what you need.

## What Apple Health Is Adding in iOS 26.4

The headline feature is Health+, an AI-powered coaching layer that sits inside the Health app. According to reports from Bloomberg and other outlets, it will pull data from your Apple Watch, iPhone, and connected devices to give you personalized recommendations on exercise, nutrition, and sleep.

The AI coach will analyze your activity trends, sleep patterns, and health metrics to offer tailored guidance — not just raw numbers, but actual suggestions. Think less "you walked 6,342 steps today" and more "your sleep quality has dropped this week — here are some adjustments that might help."

Apple is also adding native nutrition tracking for the first time. You'll be able to log meals, count calories, and track macronutrients directly in the Health app without a third-party tool. For anyone who's been using MyFitnessPal or Lose It just for food logging, this could simplify things significantly.

Rounding it out: expert-led health videos from sleep specialists, nutritionists, cardiologists, and physical therapists, plus a workout form analysis feature that uses your iPhone camera to give real-time feedback on your exercise technique.

It's a meaningful step forward. Apple Health has always been good at collecting data — now it's starting to help you understand it.

## Where Apple Health Still Has Gaps

Here's the thing about Apple Health, even with the update: it's built around fitness and wellness metrics. Steps, heart rate, sleep, nutrition, workouts. If your health picture fits neatly into those categories, the new AI coach might be all you need.

But a lot of people's health lives don't fit that mold.

**Medications.** Apple Health doesn't track your medications in any meaningful way. There's no place to log what you take, set reminders for doses, or see how your medications relate to your symptoms over time. If you're managing prescriptions — especially multiple ones — that's a significant gap.

**Symptoms.** You can log some health metrics manually, but Apple Health isn't designed for the kind of daily symptom tracking that people with chronic conditions need. There's no built-in way to record pain levels, brain fog, digestive issues, or energy fluctuations and then see trends over weeks and months.

**Health records context.** Apple Health Records lets you pull in clinical data from participating hospitals, which is useful. But you can't upload your own documents — lab results from an independent lab, a specialist's notes, a prescription change your doctor mentioned over the phone. The system is tethered to what participating institutions choose to share.

**AI that knows your full story.** The new AI coach will be impressive for fitness and nutrition guidance. But it won't know about your medication history, your recent lab results, or the symptoms you've been tracking. It can't connect the dots between "your sleep got worse this month" and "you switched blood pressure medications three weeks ago" — because it doesn't have access to that second piece of information.

## What Standalone Health Companions Add

This is where dedicated health apps fill a different need. They're not competing with Apple Health on step counting or workout analysis — they're solving problems Apple Health wasn't designed to address.

Apps like **CareClinic** offer structured, protocol-based tracking for specific conditions, with standardized mental health assessments and evidence-based care plans. **Bearable** excels at surfacing correlations between dozens of health variables, helping people with chronic conditions identify triggers and patterns. **heymedy** takes a different approach as an AI health companion that brings medications, symptoms, and health records into one place — with an AI that can answer questions about your personal health data because it actually has the full picture.

The key difference isn't features on a checklist. It's context. Apple Health's AI will know your fitness data extremely well. A standalone health companion can know your medications, your symptoms, your uploaded lab results, and your health history — and connect all of those to give you answers that a fitness-focused AI simply can't.

## So, Do You Need Both?

For many people, the honest answer is: Apple Health handles one part of your health life and a standalone companion handles another.

If you're generally healthy and want better insight into your fitness, sleep, and nutrition, Apple Health's new AI features may be enough. Apple does hardware integration better than anyone, and having all of that analysis built into your phone without extra apps is genuinely convenient.

But if you take medications, track symptoms, manage a chronic condition, or want your lab results and doctor's notes accessible alongside your daily health data — Apple Health's update doesn't change the fact that it wasn't built for those use cases.

The two aren't in competition so much as they cover different territory. Apple Health can tell you how well you slept and how active you were. An app like heymedy can tell you whether your new medication is helping, what patterns to mention at your next appointment, and pull up your last blood work when you need it.

Think of it this way: Apple Health is a great fitness and wellness dashboard. A standalone health companion is your personal health record that actually talks back.

The best setup might be both — let Apple Health handle what it's great at, and use a dedicated companion for everything else. They're solving different problems, and your health deserves answers to all of them.
